A parcel goes missing between a sorting centre and a doorstep. Nobody at your company touched it. The scan history is a matter of public record and it exonerates you completely.

The one-star review still has your name on it.

## The customer never signed a contract with the carrier

Think about what a shopper actually sees. They chose your brand, paid your checkout, got an email from your address, and waited for a box with your logo on it. The carrier appears once, as a courier name on a tracking page, usually in grey text under a barcode.

You have a commercial relationship with that carrier. Your customer does not. So when the box fails to arrive, there is only one party in the story they can name, and it isn't the one that lost it.

This isn't customers being unfair. It's customers being reasonable about the only relationship they are actually in.

## Why explaining whose fault it is makes things worse

The instinct is to explain. Forward the tracking, point at the scan, note that the carrier marked it delivered, suggest they check with a neighbour. Every sentence of that's true and every sentence of it reads as a company declining to help.

Worse, it puts the customer to work. They now have to contact a carrier they have no account with, quote a number they don't understand, and wait on a queue for a company that has no reason to prioritise them. You have handed a person who is already out of pocket a second job.

The fault genuinely isn't yours. Establishing that in front of the customer buys you nothing, because they weren't really asking who was at fault. They were asking whether they'd been abandoned.

## Own the resolution, not the blame

The move that works is separating the two questions. Fault is a matter for you and the carrier, settled later, in a claims process the customer never sees. Resolution is a matter for you and the customer, settled now.

Practically that means the first reply is a decision rather than an investigation. Replacement dispatched, or refund issued, or the exchange they'd rather have. The carrier conversation happens afterwards, on your side of the wall, where it belongs.

Merchants resist this because it feels like paying for someone else's mistake. Sometimes it is. But the alternative is paying for it anyway, in a lost customer and a public review, without getting any credit for the payment.

## What this looks like when it is set up properly

The customer reports a problem once, in your brand, on your domain. They are not asked to prove anything unreasonable, and they are certainly not asked to contact the carrier. They get an answer while the problem is still fresh rather than a case number.

Behind that, somebody is filing the carrier claim, tracking the recovery and absorbing the cost that cannot be recovered. That work is real and it's got to be done by someone. The only question is whether it lands on your support team or somewhere else.

The customer never sees any of it. That's the point. What they see is a brand that fixed a problem it did not cause, which is a considerably better story than a brand that proved the problem wasn't its fault.
